## Cold open

Hey everyone, and welcome back to a channel about history's biggest disasters, and the tiny, stupid mistakes that started them.
Last time, a man who didn't read his memo accidentally opened the Berlin Wall, and for once, the story ended with a party.
I promised you the next one wouldn't, so let's get this over with.
It's December seventh, nineteen forty-one, two minutes past seven on a quiet Sunday morning in Hawaii.
Two young privates on a hilltop are staring at the biggest blip they have ever seen on a radar screen, and it is heading straight for them.
So they do exactly what they're supposed to do, and they pick up the phone.
And the officer on the other end of the line listens, thinks about it, and says: don't worry about it.
A couple of hours later, more than two thousand Americans are dead, and the United States is at war.

And here's the part that makes it worse.
That phone call wasn't even the first warning that morning, and it wouldn't be the last.
So how do you sleep through three alarms in a single morning?
To answer that, we need to talk about oil.

## Running on empty

By nineteen forty-one, Japan had been fighting a brutal war in China for four years, and it had just marched into French Indochina as well.
The United States answered by freezing Japanese money and cutting off its oil, which was a big deal, because most of Japan's oil came from America.
Without oil, Japan's navy would slowly turn into a very expensive collection of parked boats.
So Japan's leaders had a choice: back down, or grab the oil fields of Southeast Asia and fight whoever tried to stop them.

They chose to fight, and the one thing that could stop them was sitting in the middle of the Pacific.
The year before, the United States had kept its Pacific Fleet at Pearl Harbor, in Hawaii, instead of California, to make Japan think twice.
Japan's admirals did think twice, and their conclusion was: sink it.

## Paradise, Sunday edition

On November twenty-sixth, Japan time, six Japanese aircraft carriers slipped out of a foggy bay in the far north of Japan and headed east, in total radio silence.
The next day, Washington sent the Navy in Hawaii a message that literally began with the words: this dispatch is to be considered a war warning.
The Army got its own message, saying hostile action was possible at any moment.
That sounds pretty clear, but the Army commander in Hawaii, General Walter Short, decided the real danger was sabotage by spies on the island.
So to protect his planes from saboteurs, he had them parked close together, wingtip to wingtip, out in the open, with the ammunition locked away.
Which is a great way to stop one guy with a wrench, and a terrible way to stop an air raid.

And on Sunday mornings, Pearl Harbor did what most of America did on Sunday mornings, which was sleep in.
Battleships lined up in a neat row, sailors on weekend leave, and a radar system that, on paper, only had to work from four to seven in the morning.
Everyone had set their alarm, so to speak.
They just weren't planning to get up.

## The first alarm

At six thirty-seven that morning, the old American destroyer Ward spotted a tiny submarine trying to sneak into the harbor behind a supply ship.
The Ward opened fire, hit the submarine, and dropped depth charges on it, and those were the first American shots of the Pacific War, fired more than an hour before the attack.
Its captain radioed the news up the chain of command, and that's where it went to slow down.
The Navy had chased plenty of false submarine sightings before, so the report got passed along, questioned, and double-checked, one desk at a time.
In other words, don't worry about it.

## The second alarm

Up on the northern tip of the island, at a place called Opana, Privates Joseph Lockard and George Elliott were finishing the early shift on a brand-new radar set.
Their shift ended at seven, but the truck that was supposed to take them to breakfast was late, so they kept the radar running for a bit more practice.
At seven oh two, a giant blob appeared on their screen, about a hundred and thirty miles to the north.
Lockard had never seen anything that big on radar, and at first he thought the machine was broken.
It wasn't.

So they called the information center at Fort Shafter, where almost everyone had already gone to breakfast.
The call ended up with Lieutenant Kermit Tyler, a fighter pilot who was on just his second shift there, with no real training and nobody to help.
And Tyler had a theory.
A flight of American B-17 bombers was due in from California that very morning, and on his drive in, he'd heard a Honolulu radio station playing Hawaiian music all night, which a friend told him happened when bombers were flying in and used the signal to find the islands.
So he put two and two together, and got bombers.
Don't worry about it, he told them.

Nobody made clear to Tyler just how big the blip was, and nobody told the privates that bombers were expected.
To be fair, the B-17s really were coming, from roughly the same direction, and they would arrive right in the middle of the attack, unarmed and nearly out of fuel.
The two privates kept tracking the blob anyway, purely for practice, until they lost it near the island at about seven forty.
Then they finally drove off to breakfast.

## The third alarm

Meanwhile, in Washington, it was already lunchtime.
American codebreakers had cracked a Japanese message telling its diplomats to deliver a final note at exactly one o'clock in the afternoon, Washington time.
General George Marshall, the head of the Army, thought one p m on a Sunday was a strange time to schedule anything, and in Hawaii, it would be early morning.
So he wrote a warning to the Pacific commands, and told them to be on alert.

Then the Army's radio couldn't get through to Hawaii, because of static.
So the most important warning of the war went out by commercial telegram, the same way you'd send your aunt birthday wishes, and it wasn't even marked urgent.
It reached the telegraph office in Honolulu at about seven thirty-three, and since the new teletype link to the Army wasn't working yet, it went into a messenger's bag, on a motorcycle.
That messenger was still on his way when the bombs started falling, and the warning didn't reach General Short until that afternoon.

## Just before eight

At about seven fifty-five, the first wave of a hundred and eighty-three Japanese planes came over the island.
Torpedo bombers went for the neat row of battleships, and dive bombers went for the planes parked wingtip to wingtip, which, as it turns out, made them very easy to hit.
At eight oh six, a bomb broke through the deck of the battleship Arizona and set off her ammunition, and the ship exploded.
One thousand one hundred and seventy-seven men died on the Arizona alone.

A second wave followed, and by ten o'clock it was over.
Two thousand four hundred and three Americans were killed, sixty-eight of them civilians, and more than eleven hundred were wounded.
Four battleships were sunk, four more were damaged, and almost two hundred planes were destroyed, most of them on the ground.

## Japan hits snooze too

But Japan had its own tiny mistakes that morning.
The big prize, the American aircraft carriers, weren't even there, because all three of them happened to be away from the harbor.
And the Japanese pilots ignored the huge tanks of fuel oil and the repair yards, so most of the damaged ships were raised, fixed, and sent back to fight.

Then there was the declaration of war, sort of.
That note was supposed to be handed over in Washington at one o'clock, less than half an hour before the bombs.
But the long message had to be decoded and typed up at the Japanese embassy, the typing went slowly, and the diplomats arrived more than an hour late.
By the time they handed it over, Pearl Harbor had already been burning for an hour.
Which is how a surprise attack became, in American eyes, a sneak attack, and an entire country woke up very angry.

## The bill

The next day, President Roosevelt called December seventh a date which will live in infamy, and Congress declared war on Japan within the hour.
Three days later, Germany and Italy declared war on the United States, and just like that, America was in the Second World War, all the way.
Japan had wanted to knock America out of the fight, and instead, it had dragged the strongest industrial power on Earth right into it.

And no, despite all the theories, the long official investigations never found proof that anyone in Washington knew the target was Pearl Harbor and let it happen.
What they found was much more ordinary, and honestly, much scarier.
A sub report nobody rushed, a radar blip nobody believed, and a warning sent like a birthday card.

## Outro

So, did one man saying don't worry about it cause Pearl Harbor?
Not really, because six aircraft carriers were already on their way, and an hour's warning would not have stopped them.
But it could have filled those guns with ammunition, pulled those planes apart, and sent fighters into the air, and some of those two thousand four hundred lives might have been saved.
Kermit Tyler was never punished, by the way, and a Navy board later said he'd been put in a job with no training, no staff and no supervision.
He retired as a lieutenant colonel, and he spent the rest of his long life being remembered for four words.

And the Ward, the little destroyer that fired the first shot?
It was sunk exactly three years later, on December seventh, nineteen forty-four, and the ship that finished her off was commanded by the same man who had been her captain at Pearl Harbor.
History, it turns out, also has a sense of humor.
Next time, a German minister sends the most secret message of the war through America's own cables, and when people call it a fake, he stands up and says, nope, that was me.
