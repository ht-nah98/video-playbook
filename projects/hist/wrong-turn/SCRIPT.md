## Cold open

Hey everyone, and welcome to a channel about history's biggest disasters, and the tiny, stupid mistakes that started them.
Take a good look at this car, because it has just taken a wrong turn, and it's about to become the most expensive wrong turn in human history.
The young man standing five feet away has a pistol in his pocket, the driver has just slammed on the brakes, and in about two seconds, a world war begins.
Around twenty million people will die because of what happens next, and the craziest part is that it very nearly didn't happen.
Because nobody told the driver.

So let's rewind to the morning of June twenty-eighth, nineteen fourteen, and count every mistake it took to get here.
Fair warning, there are at least five of them, and each one is dumber than the last.

## The powder keg

First, a quick tour of Europe in nineteen fourteen, which looked less like a continent and more like a high school cafeteria with armies.
Britain, France and Russia sat at one table, Germany and Austria-Hungary sat at another, and Italy was technically at the German table, but already eyeing the exit.
Austria-Hungary, by the way, was one empire, two countries, about a dozen languages and one very old emperor, Franz Joseph, who had been on the throne since eighteen forty-eight.

Right next door sat Serbia, small, proud, and fresh off winning two Balkan wars in two years.
Serbia dreamed of uniting the South Slavs, including the ones in Bosnia, which Austria-Hungary had occupied for thirty years and then simply annexed in nineteen oh eight.
Imagine your neighbor borrowing your lawnmower for thirty years, and then one day announcing that it's his lawnmower now.
That's roughly how Serbia felt about Bosnia, and plenty of young Bosnians felt the same way.
Meanwhile in Vienna, the head of the army kept begging for a war on Serbia.
By one count, he proposed it about twenty-five times in nineteen thirteen alone, like the uncle who pitches the same crypto scheme every Thanksgiving.
And on one Sunday morning in Sarajevo, somebody was finally going to give him his excuse.

## The unloved heir

Now, meet the man in the back seat, Archduke Franz Ferdinand, heir to the throne of Austria-Hungary.
He only became heir because two other men died first, and the old emperor never really warmed to him.
He was short-tempered, not very popular at court, and so obsessed with hunting that his records list more than two hundred and seventy thousand animals.
Averaged out, that's about fifteen animals a day, for every single day he was alive.

But he did one thing the court never forgave him for, and that was marrying for love.
His wife, Sophie Chotek, was a countess, which sounds fancy, unless you're a Habsburg, in which case a countess is practically a commoner.
To be allowed to marry her, Franz Ferdinand had to swear that their children would never inherit the throne.
In Vienna, Sophie couldn't sit next to her husband in the royal box, and she couldn't ride beside him in a royal carriage.
But in Bosnia he was visiting as the head of the army, not as a royal, so for once Sophie could sit right next to him in an open car.
Which is sweet, and it's also the only reason she was in that car at all.

Mistake number one was the date.
They would drive through Sarajevo on June twenty-eighth, which for Serbs is Vidovdan, the anniversary of a famous battle in thirteen eighty-nine and one of the most emotional days of their year.
So the heir of the empire that had just grabbed Bosnia picked Serbian patriotism's biggest holiday to parade through Bosnia's capital.
It's a bit like throwing a Yankees victory parade through downtown Boston, on the one day everybody there is already in a terrible mood.
For the couple, though, it was an anniversary, because on that exact date fourteen years earlier, Franz Ferdinand had sworn away his children's rights so he could marry Sophie.
Which made it a lovely day for a drive, and a perfect day for an assassination.

## Six assassins

Waiting for them were six young men from a movement called Young Bosnia, five Bosnian Serbs and one Bosnian Muslim, and most of them were still teenagers.
They had four pistols, six bombs, and little vials of cyanide, so that after the attack they could kill themselves instead of talking.
Where does a group of broke teenagers get all that?
From the Black Hand, a secret society run by officers inside the Serbian army, whose leader, nicknamed Apis, also happened to be the head of Serbian military intelligence.
To be clear, the Serbian government didn't order this, and Serbia's prime minister may even have heard about it and tried to stop it.
But when the head of your spies runs an assassination club on the side, it's not a great look.
The weapons were smuggled across the border, and in Sarajevo the six spread out along the main road by the river, each one waiting for his moment.

## The plan falls apart

Mistake number two was security, which was about a hundred and twenty local police for a route lined with crowds.
And in case any assassin missed it, the route had been printed in the newspaper.
A little before ten in the morning, the motorcade rolled along the river, with the archduke and Sophie in the third car and the top folded down so everyone could get a good look.

The first assassin saw the car coming, and did nothing, and neither did the second one.
The third, Nedeljko Chabrinovich, reportedly asked a policeman which car the archduke was in, got a helpful answer, knocked the cap off his bomb against a lamppost and threw it.
The bomb had a fuse of about ten seconds, so it bounced off the folded roof, rolled into the street and exploded under the next car, wounding nearly twenty people.

Chabrinovich then swallowed his cyanide and jumped into the river, which is a very dramatic exit.
Except the cyanide was so old it only made him throw up, and the river, in June, was about five inches deep.
So instead of a heroic death, he got a stomachache, wet pants, and a beating from the crowd before the police dragged him away.
The motorcade sped off, the rest of the assassins froze or lost their chance, and Gavrilo Princip, nineteen years old, watched the car he'd come to kill race straight past him.

At this point, the plan had completely failed.
Archduke safe, assassins scattered, World War One cancelled.

## But no

But no.
The motorcade reached the town hall, where the mayor, determined to stick to the script, began reading his welcome speech about how happy Sarajevo was to see its beloved guest.
Franz Ferdinand cut him off, furious.
"Mister Mayor, I come here on a visit, and I am greeted with bombs! It is outrageous!"
Sophie whispered something to calm him down, and the mayor went right back to his speech.
Then it was the archduke's turn, and since his notes had been riding in the car that got bombed, he read them out spattered with the blood of one of his own officers.

Mistake number three was that nobody called off the rest of the day.
Instead, Franz Ferdinand decided to go and visit the people wounded by the bomb, in the hospital.
Someone asked if that was really safe, and the governor in charge of security, Oskar Potiorek, reportedly answered, "Do you think Sarajevo is full of assassins?"
At that moment, Sarajevo contained five more armed assassins, but sure, that was mistake number four.
To play it safe, they changed the plan, and instead of turning into the narrow streets of the old town, the cars would drive straight along the river to the hospital.
It was a sensible plan, and it had just one small problem.
Mistake number five: nobody told the driver.

## The wrong turn

At about ten forty-five, the motorcade set off again, and the first car, carrying the mayor, turned right off the river road into Franz Joseph Street, exactly like the old plan said.
The archduke's driver, Leopold Lojka, knew nothing about the new plan, so he followed it.
For the record, he was the only man in this story doing exactly what he'd been told.
Potiorek, sitting right there in the car, shouted that this was the wrong way and that they were supposed to stay on the river road.
The driver hit the brakes to back up, and the car came to a stop right in front of Moritz Schiller's delicatessen.
And standing right there, about five feet away, was Gavrilo Princip, who had given up for the day and wandered over to that corner.
And no, he wasn't there to buy a sandwich, because that story only appeared almost a century later.

## Two shots

Princip stepped forward and fired twice.
One bullet hit Franz Ferdinand in the neck, and the other hit Sophie in the abdomen.
Franz Ferdinand turned to his wife and begged her, "Sophie, don't die, stay alive for our children."
When someone asked if he was in pain, he kept repeating, "It is nothing," more quietly each time.
Before eleven thirty that morning, both of them were dead.
Princip tried to turn the pistol on himself, but the crowd seized him first.

## Five weeks

Here's the strange part, though, because back then, assassinations weren't exactly rare.
In the twenty years before Sarajevo, assassins had killed an American president, a king of Italy, a king of Greece, and Franz Joseph's own wife, and none of those started a world war.
What made this one different was what the great powers decided to do with it.

Austria-Hungary saw its chance to finally crush Serbia, but first it needed to know that if Russia, Serbia's big protector, jumped in, Germany would have its back.
So on July fifth, Austria's ambassador had lunch with Kaiser Wilhelm the Second, and the Kaiser basically told him, do whatever you need to do, we'll support you.
Historians call this the blank check, the diplomatic version of handing your friend your credit card and saying, "Don't worry about it."
The next day, the Kaiser left on his summer cruise to Norway, like you do.

On July twenty-third, Austria-Hungary sent Serbia an ultimatum full of demands designed to be rejected, and gave it forty-eight hours to answer.
Serbia, to almost everyone's surprise, accepted almost every demand, some with conditions, and refused only the part about Austrian officials running an investigation on Serbian soil.
Even the Kaiser, back from his cruise, read the reply and wrote that every reason for war had now disappeared.
Austria-Hungary declared war anyway, on July twenty-eighth, exactly one month after Sarajevo, and it did it by telegram.

## Dominoes

And then the cafeteria did exactly what cafeterias do, because one kid threw a punch, and every table stood up.
Russia began mobilizing its army to protect Serbia, so Germany declared war on Russia, and then on Russia's ally France.
To get to France quickly, Germany marched straight through Belgium, which was neutral and very much not okay with it.
Britain had promised to protect Belgium in a treaty from eighteen thirty-nine, which the German chancellor dismissed as "a scrap of paper", and on August fourth, Britain declared war on Germany.

The ridiculous part is that the Kaiser, the Tsar and the King of Britain were all cousins.
In those final days, Wilhelm and Nicholas sent each other telegrams in English, signed Willy and Nicky, politely begging each other not to start a war.
It didn't work, and about five weeks after one wrong turn, almost all of Europe's great powers were at war.
Crowds cheered in the streets of Berlin, Paris and London, and everyone agreed it would all be over by Christmas.

It would not be over by Christmas.

Although, weirdly, the archduke's car may have already known when it would be.
Its license plate read A, then the Roman numeral three, then one, one, eight, and the internet likes to read that as Armistice, eleven, eleven, eighteen.
That's pure coincidence, of course, but November eleventh, nineteen eighteen, is exactly the day the war ended.

## The bill

The war lasted more than four years.
Around twenty million people died, soldiers and civilians, roughly everyone living in New York State today.
Four empires, the German, the Austro-Hungarian, the Russian and the Ottoman, did not survive it.
And the peace that ended it left so much anger behind that it helped set the stage for an even bigger war only twenty years later.

Gavrilo Princip never saw the end of it.
Because he was under twenty, he couldn't be executed, so he was sentenced to twenty years in prison.
He died of tuberculosis in April nineteen eighteen, about seven months before the war he had helped start was finally over.

## Outro

So what really started World War One?
It wasn't just the driver, of course, because it took empires, alliances, nationalism and a lot of powerful men who were perfectly happy to have a war.
But on that one morning, five tiny mistakes lined up perfectly: the date, the security, the decision to carry on, the governor's confidence, and one change of plan that never reached the front seat.
Change any one of them, and maybe the day ends with a boring speech and a hospital visit.
Instead, nobody told the driver.

History's biggest disasters started with tiny, stupid mistakes, and on this channel we're going to find every one of them.
So stick around, because next time, the mistake is even smaller, and the disaster is even bigger.
