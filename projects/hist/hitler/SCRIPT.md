## Cold open

Hey everyone, and welcome back to a channel about history's biggest disasters, and the tiny, stupid mistakes that started them.
Today is a little different, because today's disaster isn't one mistake, it's one man who simply could not stop making them.
It's June sixth, nineteen forty-four, D-Day, and more than a hundred and fifty thousand Allied soldiers are coming ashore in Normandy.
Germany has powerful tank divisions waiting in reserve, close enough to throw those soldiers back into the sea, but there's a catch.
Only one person in the entire country is allowed to send them, and that person is in bed.
His staff look at the confusing reports, decide this is probably just a trick, and agree that the boss can sleep in.

So the big tank reserves wait, the morning passes, and by the time they finally move, the Allies are on the beach to stay.
And here's the thing: that wasn't Adolf Hitler's first big mistake.
It wasn't even close.
So let's go back to the beginning, to a young man in Vienna with a folder full of paintings, who is about to be told no.

## The art student

In nineteen-oh-seven, an eighteen-year-old Hitler moved to Vienna to become a great artist, and he applied to the Academy of Fine Arts.
He passed the first round, but then he failed the drawing test, and the examiners wrote a short note in their register: test drawing unsatisfactory, few heads.
In other words, he could draw buildings just fine, he just wasn't very good at people, which, looking back, explains a lot.

A year later he tried again, and this time they didn't even let him sit the exam.
He later claimed the director told him he should study architecture instead, but architecture school required a diploma, and Hitler had dropped out of high school.
So he stayed in Vienna, sleeping in cheap hostels and selling small watercolors of famous buildings through local dealers, like a very angry postcard shop.
Now, it's tempting to say that if one art school had just said yes, the whole twentieth century would have turned out fine.
That's a joke, not history, but it's a joke historians have heard about a thousand times.

## The beer hall

After fighting in the First World War, Hitler ended up in Munich, where he discovered that the one thing he was actually brilliant at was shouting at crowds.
He took over a small, angry party, and by nineteen twenty-three he thought he was ready to take over the whole country.
So on the evening of November eighth, he burst into a packed beer hall, fired a pistol into the ceiling, and announced that the revolution had begun.
His plan was to force three of Bavaria's most powerful men to join him, then march on Berlin, the way Mussolini had just marched on Rome.

The three men agreed, and then, as soon as Hitler let them leave, they took it all back.
The next morning, about two thousand of his followers marched through Munich anyway, ran straight into a line of police, and the shooting started.
About sixteen of his men and four policemen were killed, and a bystander died too, and Hitler ran away and was arrested two days later.
At his trial he gave long speeches, the newspapers printed every word, and the judge, who was openly on his side, gave him the lightest sentence he could, five years.
He served about nine months, in a surprisingly comfortable prison, with visitors and gifts, where he wrote a book called Mein Kampf.
So his first attempt to seize power was a complete disaster, and somehow it made him famous.

## Not elected

He learned one lesson from the beer hall: don't storm the government, get invited into it.
So for the next ten years he played politics, and when the Great Depression hit Germany, his party started winning votes, a lot of votes.
But here's a fact that surprises a lot of people: Hitler never won a majority in a free election.
In nineteen thirty-two he ran for president and lost, and his party's best result was thirty-seven percent.
Then a group of conservative politicians decided they could use him, put him in charge as chancellor, and keep him under control.
That was the plan, in January nineteen thirty-three.
Within two months he had passed a law that let him rule without parliament, and within two years, nobody was controlling anybody.

## The halt order

In September nineteen thirty-nine, Germany invaded Poland, Britain and France declared war, and the Second World War began.
Then, in May nineteen forty, Germany did something nobody expected, sending its tanks through a thick, hilly forest the French were sure no big tank army could get through quickly, and smashing into France.
In just two weeks, most of the British army in France, together with many French soldiers, was trapped against the sea at a port called Dunkirk.
German tanks were only about fifteen miles away, and Britain was about to lose its whole army.
And then the tanks stopped.

The order to halt came from one of his generals, who wanted to rest his tanks, but Hitler approved it, and overruled the generals who wanted to keep going.
Hermann Göring, the head of the air force, promised that his planes could finish the job alone, which was a very Göring thing to promise.
For about three days the tanks waited, and on the other side of the line, the British got to work.
Destroyers, ferries, fishing boats and private yachts crossed the Channel again and again, and in nine days they carried three hundred and thirty-eight thousand soldiers home.
Britain lost almost all of its equipment, but it kept its army, and an army can always get new trucks.

## Changing targets

That summer, Hitler wanted to invade Britain, but first he needed to destroy the Royal Air Force, so the bombers went after its airfields and radar stations.
It was hurting, and by late August, the British air force was badly stretched.
Then, one night, a few German bombs fell on London, probably by accident, and Britain bombed Berlin in return.
Hitler was furious, and partly out of revenge, he ordered his air force to stop hitting the airfields and start hitting London instead.

It was a horror for the people of London, who were bombed night after night for months.
But it also gave the exhausted British pilots time to repair their airfields, get some rest and fight back.
By the middle of September, Germany had lost so many planes that Hitler quietly postponed the invasion of Britain, and it never happened.

## Kicking the door

So Hitler couldn't beat Britain, and his solution was to attack the biggest country on Earth.
On June twenty-second, nineteen forty-one, more than three million soldiers poured into the Soviet Union, along a front nearly two thousand miles long.
Hitler reportedly said that they only had to kick in the door, and the whole rotten structure would come crashing down.
And at first, it looked like he might be right, because entire Soviet armies were surrounded and destroyed in the first few months.

But the plan assumed the war would be over before winter, so a lot of German soldiers didn't have winter clothes.
The roads turned to mud in the autumn, the supply lines stretched for hundreds of miles, and the Soviets kept finding new armies.
By early December, German soldiers were about twenty miles from Moscow, in temperatures far below freezing.
And then the Soviet counterattack hit them, with fresh troops from the east, dressed for the snow.
The door, it turned out, was attached to a very large house.

## The serious part

Before we go any further, we need to be clear about what this war was actually for.
Behind the front lines, Hitler's regime and its collaborators carried out the murder of about six million Jewish men, women and children.
They were shot beside pits, starved in ghettos, and killed in death camps like Auschwitz.
Millions of others were murdered too, including Soviet prisoners of war, Polish civilians, Roma, and disabled people.
There is nothing funny about any of it, and nothing in this video is a joke about them.
The jokes are about one thing only: how a man responsible for all this kept making the decisions that destroyed him.

## Four days later

On December seventh, nineteen forty-one, Japan attacked Pearl Harbor, and the United States entered the war against Japan.
Germany had a treaty with Japan, but it only required Germany to help if Japan was attacked, and Japan had very much done the attacking.
So Hitler was under no obligation to do anything at all.
Four days later, he stood up in front of his parliament and declared war on the United States anyway.

At this point, Germany was fighting Britain and the Soviet Union, and was stuck in the snow outside Moscow.
And now it had added the largest industrial economy in the world, a country that would soon be building tanks, ships and planes faster than anybody had ever built anything.
If you're keeping track, that's three of the most powerful countries on the planet, against Germany, and the last one he picked himself.

## The airlift

In the summer of nineteen forty-two, Hitler sent his armies south, toward the oil fields of the Caucasus and a city on the Volga River that happened to carry Stalin's name.
Stalingrad turned into months of street fighting, building by building and room by room.
In November, the Soviets launched a huge attack on both sides of the city, and in just four days, nearly three hundred thousand German soldiers and their allies were surrounded.
His generals wanted to break out while they still could, but Hitler said no, and Göring, once again, made a promise.

He said his air force could keep the whole army supplied from the air.
The army needed around seven hundred and fifty tons of supplies every single day, and on average, the planes delivered about a hundred.
So the soldiers starved and froze, and at the very end, Hitler promoted their commander, Friedrich Paulus, to field marshal, because no German field marshal had ever surrendered.
Paulus surrendered the next day.
About ninety-one thousand Germans were taken prisoner, and only about five or six thousand of them ever made it home.

## Do not disturb

Which brings us back to where we started, June sixth, nineteen forty-four.
For months, the Allies had been feeding Germany fake radio traffic, a fake army, and even inflatable tanks, to make it look like the invasion would come at Calais.
So when the landings began in Normandy, Hitler's staff decided it was probably a diversion, and they let him sleep.
Germany's most famous general, Erwin Rommel, the man in charge of the beach defenses, wasn't there either, because he had gone home for his wife's birthday.

The tank reserves finally got permission to move in the afternoon, and when they did, Allied planes caught them on the roads.
Two months later, Paris was free, and Germany was being squeezed from the east and the west at the same time.

## The bunker

By the spring of nineteen forty-five, Hitler was living underground, in a concrete bunker beneath Berlin, moving armies around on a map, armies that mostly no longer existed.
Soviet troops were fighting their way through the streets above him.
On April thirtieth, nineteen forty-five, he killed himself, and about a week later, Germany surrendered.
The war he started killed tens of millions of people, and most of Europe lay in ruins.

## The ranking

So, which was his worst mistake?
Down at the bottom, we'll put the art school, because honestly, that one wasn't his fault, his drawings were just a bit boring.
The beer hall goes low too, because his failure there made him famous.
Changing targets in the Battle of Britain is a solid mistake, and the halt at Dunkirk, which let the British army escape, is even worse.
Sleeping in on D-Day is embarrassing, but by then the war was already lost.
And at the very top, two decisions made within six months of each other: invading the Soviet Union, and declaring war on the United States, when absolutely nothing forced him to.
That's the moment the most dangerous man of the century decided to fight the whole world at once.

## Outro

The people who stopped him did it with enormous courage and terrible sacrifice, and they deserve every bit of the credit.
But they were also helped, again and again, by a man who believed he was a genius, and who never, ever listened to anyone who told him no.
Except, maybe, that art school.
