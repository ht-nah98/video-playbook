## Cold open

Hey everyone, and welcome back to a channel about history's biggest disasters, and the tiny, stupid mistakes that started them.
Last time, one driver took one wrong turn and started a world war, and I promised you that the next mistake would be even smaller.
Well, here it is, and it's a single sheet of paper.
It's November ninth, nineteen eighty-nine, in East Berlin, and the tired man at that table is reading a memo out loud on live television.
His boss handed it to him right before he walked in, and he has barely looked at it.
A reporter asks him one simple question, when does this start, and he shuffles his papers, squints, and says, as far as he knows, immediately.
By midnight, the Berlin Wall is open.
Within a year, his entire country no longer exists.

So how does one unread piece of paper beat twenty-eight years of concrete?
To answer that, we need to go back to the moment a city got cut in half.

## A city cut in half

After World War Two, the winners split Germany into four zones, and then, because Berlin sat deep inside the Soviet zone, they split Berlin into four as well.
It was like four roommates sharing one apartment, except the apartment was in the middle of a house that only one of them lived in.
Pretty soon the roommates stopped getting along, the American, British and French zones became West Germany, and the Soviet zone became East Germany.
West Berlin was left as a little island of capitalism, about a hundred miles inside a communist country.

And East Germans kept doing something their government really hated, which was leaving.
Before nineteen sixty-one, about three and a half million of them left, roughly one in every five people in the country, and many of them simply took the subway to West Berlin.
Imagine running a country where your citizens can quit by catching a train.

## Barbed Wire Sunday

So at midnight on August thirteenth, nineteen sixty-one, police and soldiers sealed the border with West Berlin while the city slept.
They tore up streets and rolled out barbed wire first, and a few days later the concrete started going up.
Berliners still call that day Barbed Wire Sunday.
Families woke up on opposite sides of a fence, and some of them wouldn't be able to visit each other for twenty-eight years.

The government didn't call it a wall, of course.
Officially it was the Anti-Fascist Protection Rampart, which is a very long name for a thing that only pointed in one direction.
Over the years it grew into a whole system, two walls with a strip of open ground between them, watchtowers, lights and armed guards, and people called that strip the death strip.
More than a hundred thousand East Germans tried to escape across the border anyway, and over five thousand made it over the Wall in Berlin.
At least a hundred and forty people died at the Wall, about a hundred of them while trying to escape, some of them teenagers, shot within sight of the West.

## Tear down this wall

For decades, Western leaders came to Berlin to stand next to the Wall and say something memorable about it.
In June nineteen eighty-seven, Ronald Reagan stood in front of the Brandenburg Gate and said, "Mister Gorbachev, tear down this wall!"
It was a great line, and the Wall did not move an inch.
In January nineteen eighty-nine, East Germany's leader, Erich Honecker, said that as long as the reasons for it remained, the Wall would still be standing in fifty and even a hundred years.
He was off by about ninety-nine years and two months.

## The summer everything leaked

Because nineteen eighty-nine was a very bad year to be a wall.
The Soviet Union under Gorbachev had stopped promising to send tanks every time a neighbor got ideas, and the neighbors noticed.
In May, Hungary started taking down the fence on its border with Austria, and in August, at an event called the Pan-European Picnic, hundreds of East Germans on holiday simply walked through an open gate into Austria.
Word got around, and tens of thousands of East Germans suddenly discovered a deep personal interest in Hungarian vacations.

Others climbed into the garden of the West German embassy in Prague, until thousands of people were camping in it.
On September thirtieth, West Germany's foreign minister stepped onto the embassy balcony to tell them they could leave for the West, and the crowd's cheering drowned out the rest of his sentence.

Inside East Germany, people stopped leaving and started marching.
In Leipzig, every Monday evening, the crowds got bigger, and on October ninth, around seventy thousand people marched around the city chanting, "We are the people."
The police and army had been given permission to use force, and there were rumors that hospitals had stocked up on extra blood.
But nobody gave the order to shoot, and nobody fired.
Nine days later, Honecker, the man with the hundred-year forecast, was pushed out, and a younger party official named Egon Krenz took over.

## The memo

Krenz needed to let some steam out, fast.
So he asked the Interior Ministry for a new travel law, and on November sixth they published a draft that was full of so many conditions and delays that West Berlin's mayor called it complete trash.
Back to the drawing board.
On the morning of November ninth, officials at the Interior Ministry, working with the Stasi, the secret police, rushed out a new rule that let people apply to travel and cross at any checkpoint, including into West Berlin.
The leadership approved it that afternoon, and it was meant to take effect the next day, November tenth, with the news held back until the next morning.

Now here's the guy who had to announce it, Günter Schabowski, the party's spokesman.
Schabowski hadn't helped write the new rule, and he wasn't in the room while the leadership talked it over.
Shortly before his evening press conference, Krenz handed him a note about the changes, with no instructions on how to handle it.
Schabowski later said he thought the job was simple, just read the text out without any mistakes.
So he walked into a room full of the world's press with a piece of paper he didn't fully understand, which, to be fair, is also how I got through college.

## The press conference

The press conference started at six in the evening, live on East German television and radio, and for about fifty minutes it was spectacularly boring.
Committees, reforms, more committees.
Then, at about six fifty-three, an Italian reporter asked about the travel law, and Schabowski remembered his note.
He read it out, stumbling over the words, and the room started buzzing, because what he was reading sounded a lot like East Germans could just leave.
A reporter asked the obvious question, when does this take effect?
Schabowski shuffled through his papers, looked for an answer that wasn't there, and said this.
"As far as I know, it takes effect immediately, without delay."
Nobody had told him about November tenth.
Nobody had told him to wait until morning.
Nobody read the memo, and the only man reading it out loud was reading it properly for the very first time.

Someone asked if this included crossings into West Berlin, and he checked the paper again, found point three, and said yes, it did.
Then a British reporter asked the question that actually mattered, what happens to the Berlin Wall now?
Schabowski gave a long, wandering answer about disarmament, and at seven o'clock he wrapped it up.
Then he sat down with an American TV anchor, Tom Brokaw of NBC, and said it all again.

## Nobody read the memo

At four minutes past seven, a West German news agency put out the headline that East Germans could cross the border immediately.
At seven seventeen it was on West German TV, and at eight it led the main evening news, which reached most of East Germany anyway.
Later that night, one of West Germany's most respected news anchors looked into the camera and said this.
"The gates in the Wall stand open wide."
They did not.
At that moment, the gates were still shut, and nobody had ordered the guards standing at them to open anything.
So now the entire world was reporting a memo that nobody had actually read.

## Bornholmer Street

But East Berliners had heard "immediately", and they took it literally.
They grabbed their coats and their ID cards and walked to the checkpoints, a few hundred at first, and then thousands.
The biggest crowd gathered at the Bornholmer Street crossing, where the officer in charge was a Stasi lieutenant colonel named Harald Jäger.
Jäger had heard the news too, and he had exactly the same question as everyone else, which was, what on earth was he supposed to do?
He called his superiors, again and again.
Their best idea was to let the loudest troublemakers out and quietly mark their passports so they could never come back, without telling them.
It didn't help, because the crowd kept growing and chanting, "Open the gate!"

Jäger's guards were armed, the crowd was enormous, and nobody above him was willing to give a real order.
So at around eleven thirty that night, without any order at all, Harald Jäger told his men to open the gate.
Thousands of people poured across the bridge into West Berlin, and barely anyone checked their papers.
Across the city, the other checkpoints gave up one by one, and West Berliners met the crowds with hugs, flowers and bottles of champagne.
By the early hours, people were standing on top of the Wall itself, the same Wall where guards had been shooting people earlier that year, and some of them started chipping away at it with hammers.

## The bill

By the next morning, there was simply no way to close the gates again.
Less than eleven months later, on October third, nineteen ninety, East Germany ceased to exist, and Germany became one country again.
A little over a year after that, the Soviet Union itself was gone.

As for Schabowski, he was expelled from the party he had served all his life.
In nineteen ninety-seven a German court convicted him, along with Krenz, for the killings of East Germans who had tried to flee, and he was sentenced to three years in prison.
Unlike most of his old comrades, he accepted moral guilt and openly denounced the system he had helped run, and he was pardoned after about a year.
He died in Berlin in twenty fifteen, best remembered for a few words he never meant to say.

## Outro

So, did one man's mistake bring down the Berlin Wall?
Not really, because the people of East Germany had been pushing on that Wall for months, in Hungary, in Prague and in the streets of Leipzig, and sooner or later something was going to give.
But the memo decided when.
It turned a careful plan for an orderly announcement the next morning into a stampede that very night, and once the gates were open, nobody could close them again.
All because nobody read the memo, and one tired man said, as far as he knew, immediately.

History's biggest disasters started with tiny, stupid mistakes, and this time, for once, the mistake turned out to be a happy ending.
So stick around, because next time, the mistake is still small, and the ending is not nearly as happy.
