## Cold open

If you spend enough time on social media, sooner or later you will meet a little sum that looks like nothing special, with thousands of people fighting about it in the comments.
The classic one goes like this: six divided by two, times the bracket one plus two. What is it?

Half the internet swears the answer is nine. The other half is just as sure it is one.
And the funny part is that both sides are confident they followed the very same rule. PEMDAS.

You probably learned it in primary school, as the order of operations.
Brackets first, then powers, then multiplication and division, and addition and subtraction last of all.

So today, we are going to ask where that order actually comes from.
Is it an unbreakable truth of mathematics, like one plus one making two?
Or is it a rule of the game, something people sat down and agreed on, simply because it was convenient?

Here is where I stand. PEMDAS is a convention, made by people. But a convention is not the same thing as an arbitrary choice.
And the best evidence that it is a convention, is exactly those sums the internet cannot stop arguing about.
To see why, we first have to look at what the rule really says, because a lot of us remember it slightly wrong.

# ACT ONE — What PEMDAS really says

## The letters

PEMDAS is just a string of initials.
Parentheses, Exponents, Multiplication, Division, Addition, Subtraction.
In Britain they call it BODMAS, in Canada BEDMAS. The letters change a little from country to country, but the idea stays the same.

## The part almost everyone gets wrong

Here is where many people go astray.
You see the M before the D, and it is easy to think that multiplication always comes before division.
You see the A before the S, and it is easy to think that addition always comes before subtraction.

But that is not the rule.
Multiplication and division sit on the same level, and you work through them from left to right, whichever comes first.
Addition and subtraction share a level too, and they also go from left to right.

Take ten minus three plus two.
If you believe addition has to come first, you work out three plus two, which is five, and then ten minus five, which is five.
But read it from left to right, the way the rule really says. Ten minus three is seven, and seven plus two is nine.
The answer is nine.

Or take eight divided by four, times two.
Left to right, eight divided by four is two, and two times two is four.
Do the multiplication first, because the M comes before the D, and you get four times two is eight, then eight divided by eight is one. Wrong again.

I start here because it matters.
A large share of those online fights happen simply because people misremember the levels.
Once you know that multiplication and division are equals, and addition and subtraction are equals, a lot of the famous arguments quietly disappear.

# ACT TWO — The part that really is just a choice

So which parts of PEMDAS did people simply decide, with nothing in nature forcing them?

## Left to right

The first is reading from left to right.
When two operations sit on the same level, like addition and subtraction, we agree to do the left one first. But that is purely a choice.
Arabic is written from right to left, and in principle we could just as well have agreed to read our sums in that direction.
In fact, someone did. In the nineteen sixties, a programming language called APL was built to read every expression from right to left, with no ladder of priorities at all.
And even in ordinary maths, we already break our own habit. Two to the three to the two, a tower of powers, is worked out from the top down. It comes to five hundred and twelve, not sixty-four.
No law of nature says an expression must be read from left to right.

## Brackets beat everything

The second point, and I think the most important one, is the role of brackets.
Brackets always win.
Want to add before you multiply? Just put the addition in brackets, and you're done.
Which means PEMDAS is really the rule for when we get lazy, for when we leave the brackets out to keep things short.
If you always wrote every bracket, PEMDAS would be almost useless, because the brackets would already tell you what to do first.

In other words, PEMDAS is a rule about how we write and read. It is not a property of the numbers themselves.

## Many names, one agreement

The third clue is the name itself.
PEMDAS, BODMAS, BEDMAS, a different one in every country.
The very fact that one rule goes by so many names tells you it is the product of an agreement.
It's like choosing which side of the road to drive on.
Everyone needs a shared rule, but the particular rule could have been different, and the world would still work.

## When it was agreed

As for when it happened, the history is less tidy than you might expect.
Putting multiplication before addition grew up with algebra itself, in the sixteen hundreds, as mathematicians started writing equations in symbols.
But the full rule, spelled out in schoolbooks, only settled in around the late eighteen hundreds and early nineteen hundreds, as algebra and printed textbooks spread.
And it did not settle quietly. In nineteen seventeen, an American maths journal was already debating whether multiplication should go before division.
The catchy names, like PEMDAS, came later still. The rule grew up with our need to write mathematics briefly, and all in the same way.

# ACT THREE — Not an arbitrary choice

If we stopped at "it's just a convention", we would be a little unfair to PEMDAS.
Because out of all the rules we could have picked, the one humanity settled on is a very clever one.

## Why multiply before you add

Think about why multiplication outranks addition.
The reason is polynomials, the things you write all the time in algebra. Take three x squared, plus two x, plus one.
With multiplication before addition, you can write that with no brackets at all. It is clean, and easy to read.
If addition outranked multiplication, you would need brackets everywhere just to say the same thing.
Three x squared in brackets, plus two x in brackets, plus one. Tiring just to look at.

There is a deeper reason too, called the distributive law.
When you write a times the bracket b plus c, it equals a b plus a c.
Giving multiplication the higher priority fits that law perfectly. The way we write things down mirrors the way the operations actually behave.

## The ladder

And why do powers sit above multiplication? Picture a ladder.
Multiplication is, at heart, repeated addition. Three times four is four, added three times.
A power is repeated multiplication. Four cubed is four, multiplied by itself three times.
On that ladder, the stronger the operation, the higher it sits, and the earlier it is done. It binds more tightly to the numbers around it.
Powers above multiplication, multiplication above addition.

The ladder is one way to see why the convention we chose feels so natural, and holds together so well.

## Convenient, not inevitable

So I think the fairest way to put it is this.
Out of all the conventions we could have invented, PEMDAS is close to the one that lets us write the most common expressions with the fewest brackets.
It is tuned for the things people write most, and that is why it feels natural.
But that natural feeling comes from convenience. Not from an unbreakable truth of mathematics.

# ACT FOUR — Where the convention runs out

## Back to the viral sum

Now let's go back to the sum from the start. Six divided by two, times the bracket one plus two.

Do the bracket first. One plus two is three, which leaves six divided by two, times three.
Multiplication and division are on the same level, so go from left to right.
Six divided by two is three, and three times three is nine. Nine.

But the people who say one have a point as well.
They look at that two pressed right up against the bracket, a multiplication with no sign, just a number touching a bracket.
In a lot of handwritten maths, especially in physics and engineering, this kind of glued-on multiplication is quietly treated as stronger than an ordinary division sign.
Read that way, two times three comes first, which is six, and six divided by six is one. One.

## Even the calculators disagree

Even calculators can't agree.
Older Texas Instruments models gave that glued-on multiplication the higher priority. Later models treat it like any other multiplication.
Same company, same keys, different answers.
When the people who build calculators can't agree with each other, you know the problem is not about who is smarter.

## The honest answer

The most honest answer to this sum is that it is badly written. It is ambiguous.
A good expression should never make its reader guess.
In practice, mathematicians almost never write it this way. They use a fraction bar.
Six on top and two times three underneath, or six over two, then times three, whichever they mean, and the argument is over.

That little division sign, a dot above and a dot below a line, has been fading out of serious mathematics for a long time. International standards for scientific writing even advise against using it.
And this is the point I want to underline.
If PEMDAS were an unbreakable truth of mathematics, there could be no expression where two people follow the rule correctly and get two different answers.
The fact that such expressions exist shows that the rule has gaps.

# ACT FIVE — So, natural, or agreed?

## Minus two squared

There is one small example I love, because it says everything.
In the usual mathematical convention, minus two squared means: square the two first, then attach the minus sign. So the answer is minus four.
But type exactly the same thing into Excel, and Excel gives you plus four.
Because Excel was built so that the minus sign holds on tighter than the power. It takes minus two first, and then squares it.
Python, on the other hand, says minus four.
And JavaScript refuses to answer at all. It stops with an error, and asks you to add brackets.
Same expression, different systems, different answers, all because each one ranks the operations its own way.

If the order of operations were an unbreakable truth of mathematics, the people who built these programs could not have chosen differently.

## The answer

So here is my answer to the question in the title.
PEMDAS is simply how we agreed to read and write mathematics briefly.
It is a shortcut. Brackets are the real tool.

And the lesson is a simple one.
When you write an expression that someone could misread, don't trust that everybody remembers PEMDAS the same way. Just add the brackets.
A good expression is one its reader never has to argue about.
