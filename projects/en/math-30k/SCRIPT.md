## Cold open

In twelve ninety-nine, the city of Florence banned the numbers you use every single day.
Zero was once treated as nothing at all. Negative numbers were called fictitious.
The square root of minus one was dismissed as useless.
And a man who claimed that some infinities are bigger than others was called a corrupter of youth.

Today, every one of those ideas sits quietly in a school textbook.
The maths you learned at school, from simple addition all the way to derivatives and integrals, did not just appear on its own.
Every symbol, every rule, every concept was invented, at a particular moment, inside one particular person's head.
And many of them were once seen as crazy, forbidden, or simply meaningless, long before they became something every student learns by heart.

So in this video, we'll walk along the timeline together, from the very first mark to the machine in your pocket.
We'll stop at each turning point, to see what was invented, and why it changed everything.
And the first stop is much older, and much simpler, than you might think.

# ACT ONE — Counting

## About 30,000 years ago — a scratch on a bone

The first mathematical invention was not a formula.
It was just a scratch, carved into a bone.

In the Czech Republic, archaeologists found a wolf bone with notches cut into it, about thirty thousand years old.
And in nineteen fifty, in central Africa, they found a baboon bone covered in notches arranged in groups, about twenty thousand years old.
It is called the Ishango bone.

It sounds simple, doesn't it? But this may be the biggest leap of all.
Because for the first time, people pulled the quantity apart from the things being counted.
Before, three sheep were simply three sheep.
After the notches, three sheep, three days, and three moons could all be written the same way: three marks.

That is the jump from counting things to the idea of number itself.
And in a sense, all of mathematics since then is that same move, made again and again: pulling the idea away from the stuff.

## About 3000 BC — Sumer

But notches have a problem.
If you need to record four hundred and thirty-seven sheep, you can't carve four hundred and thirty-seven lines.

About five thousand years ago, the Sumerians of Mesopotamia invented the first written numerals.
One sign for one, another for ten, and another for sixty.
They counted in base sixty, not base ten.

Why sixty?
Because sixty divides evenly by two, three, four, five, six, ten, twelve, fifteen, twenty, and thirty.
That makes it wonderful for sharing out land, grain, and time.

And here is the fun part: Sumer is still alive in your daily life.
An hour has sixty minutes, a minute has sixty seconds, and a full circle has three hundred and sixty degrees, which is six times sixty.
So every time you check a clock, you are using an invention that is five thousand years old.

## About 1800 to 1600 BC — Babylon and Egypt

The Babylonians inherited base sixty, and pushed it much further.
They solved quadratic equations, and they kept tables of multiplication.
On a small clay tablet known as Y B C seven two eight nine, a student wrote down the square root of two, correct to about one part in a million.

The Egyptians, around the same time, were just as skilled.
On the Rhind papyrus, they worked out the area of a circle with a rule that amounts to pi being about three point one six.
And they built pyramids with astonishing precision.

But both civilisations were missing one thing.
They had rules, and they trusted those rules, because the rules kept giving the right answers.
What they didn't ask was: why are the rules true?
Their mathematics was a handbook of recipes, not a system.
And this is where the Greeks walk in.

# ACT TWO — Proof

## About 300 BC — Euclid

Around three hundred BC, in Alexandria, a Greek mathematician named Euclid wrote a set of books called the Elements.
And in them, he set out a completely new way of doing mathematics.

He began with five basic assumptions about geometry, called axioms.
For example: between any two points, you can draw a straight line.
Then, from those five alone, he built the whole of plane geometry.
The angles of a triangle, Pythagoras' theorem, the properties of circles, all of it.
Every new statement was derived from the ones already proved before it.

This changed the very nature of mathematics.
After Euclid, a theorem was no longer just a rule that gives the right number.
It was a statement derived from axioms, through strict steps of logic.
And once it was proved, it was true forever, for anyone, anywhere, in any age.
The Elements was used to teach geometry for more than two thousand years.

Remember his fifth axiom, by the way. It will come back later, and it will shake everything.

# ACT THREE — The numbers grow

## 628 — Brahmagupta and zero

There is one thing that neither Greece nor Rome had: zero, as a number.

Think about it this way.
In the numbers we use today, the three in thirty and the three in three hundred mean different things, because they sit in different places.
That is exactly why you can add and multiply in columns: ones, tens, hundreds.

Roman numerals don't work like that.
An X is always ten, wherever it stands.
No places, no columns, and no number for an empty place.
So adding was awkward, and multiplying was done on your fingers, or on a counting board.

In the year six twenty-eight, the Indian mathematician Brahmagupta wrote a book called the Brahmasphutasiddhanta.
In it, for the first time in history, someone wrote down the rules for calculating with zero as a real number: adding zero, subtracting zero, multiplying by zero.
Zero was no longer just an empty space. It was a number that could take part in a calculation.

Now put that zero together with the base-ten place-value system that Indian mathematicians had already built.
What you get is the number system you use today.
The same symbol, with a value that depends on where it stands.

## About 820 — Al-Khwarizmi

Around the year eight twenty, in Baghdad, the scholar al-Khwarizmi wrote a book called al-jabr wa al-muqabala.
That word, al-jabr, is where the word algebra comes from.

So what did he actually invent?
Not symbols. He wrote everything out in words, and he simply called the unknown "the thing".
What he invented was a method.
Any equation of a given kind could be brought into one of a few standard forms, and then two moves would solve it: restoring, which is al-jabr, and balancing, which is al-muqabala.

Sound familiar?
It is exactly how you solve for x at school.
Before him, problems were solved one at a time, each with its own trick.
After him, there was one general procedure for a whole family of equations.

He also wrote a second book, on the Indian numerals.
In its Latin translation, it opens with the words "Algoritmi said", and Algoritmi is his name.
That is where the word algorithm comes from.
That book is how Europe first heard about Indian numerals, but Europe was in no hurry.

## 1202 — Fibonacci, and a ban

While the Islamic world calculated with Indian numerals, Europe stuck with Roman numerals for centuries.
Then, in twelve oh two, an Italian merchant's son, Leonardo of Pisa, known today as Fibonacci, published a book called Liber Abaci.
It taught the Hindu-Arabic numerals, along with methods for calculating by hand.
Adding was faster, multiplying was faster, and the accounts were far clearer.
Italian merchants carried the book around like a manual.

But not everyone welcomed it.
In twelve ninety-nine, Florence banned Arabic numerals in commercial books.
And the reason wasn't religion. It was fraud.
Roman numerals are hard to alter, but Arabic numerals are easy.
Add a zero at the end, and ten becomes a hundred. Add one small stroke, and a one becomes a seven.
It took until the sixteenth century for Hindu-Arabic numerals to finally replace Roman numerals across Europe.

## 1545 — Cardano: negative and imaginary

In fifteen forty-five, the Italian mathematician Gerolamo Cardano published Ars Magna, The Great Art.
It contained formulas for solving equations of degree three and degree four.
The formulas had been found by del Ferro, by Tartaglia, and by Cardano's own student Ferrari, but it was Cardano who made them public.
And along the way, two very strange things appeared.

The first was negative numbers.
Cubic equations often had negative solutions, and Cardano called them fictitious.
After all, how could there be a number smaller than nothing?
For centuries, European mathematicians kept arguing about them.
Negative numbers were only fully accepted around the eighteenth century, once people tied them to debt, to temperatures below zero, and to direction on a number line.

The second was even stranger.
Cardano ran into the square root of a negative number, and called it as subtle as it was useless.
But in fifteen seventy-two, Rafael Bombelli took it seriously.
He let these impossible roots into the calculation, and by the end, they cancelled each other out.
What was left was a real, correct answer.

The square root of minus one was later named imaginary, and given the letter i.
And for two and a half centuries, that name was taken literally.
It didn't exist. It was just a trick for calculating.

## 1585 — Stevin and decimals

In fifteen eighty-five, the Flemish engineer Simon Stevin published a thin booklet called De Thiende, The Tenth.
Before it, fractions were clumsy: one third, two sevenths, five twelfths, each written its own way.
Adding and subtracting them was a nightmare.

Stevin showed that they could all be written in one format: tenths, hundredths, thousandths.
Decimal fractions already existed in the Islamic world, but it was Stevin who made them ordinary in Europe.
Suddenly, every number sat on the same line.
They could be compared at a glance, and added, subtracted, multiplied and divided with the very same method.

A small change in form, with an enormous effect.
Every scientific, engineering, and business calculation since then runs on decimals.

# ACT FOUR — The language of change

## 1637 — Descartes: coordinates

In sixteen thirty-seven, René Descartes published La Géométrie.
It holds an idea so simple that anyone can follow it, and yet its consequences are almost endless.

Here is the idea.
Give every point in the plane a pair of numbers, usually called x and y.
Now a geometric shape, like a circle, can be written as an equation.
And the other way round, an equation can be drawn as a shape.

This joined two branches of mathematics that had grown up apart: the geometry of the Greeks, and the algebra of the Islamic world.
For the first time, they spoke one language, and the new field became known as analytic geometry.

So what changed?
Every geometry problem could now become a calculation, and every algebra problem could now become a picture.
And more importantly, it laid the foundation that Newton and Leibniz would build calculus on, only a few decades later.

## 1665 to 1684 — Newton and Leibniz: calculus

Between the sixteen sixties and the sixteen eighties, two men, working independently, invented calculus.
Isaac Newton in England, and Gottfried Leibniz in Germany.
Calculus solved two very old problems.

The first is the rate of change at a single instant.
If you know where an object is at every moment, how fast is it moving at exactly one moment?
Not its average speed over a stretch of time, but its speed right now.
The answer is the derivative.

The second is adding up infinitely many, infinitely small pieces.
To find the area under any curve, you have to add up infinitely many slices, each one vanishingly thin.
The answer is the integral.

And here is the most beautiful part.
These two operations, which look completely unrelated, turn out to undo each other.
That is the fundamental theorem of calculus.

Calculus became the language in which Newton wrote his laws of motion and gravity.
Maxwell wrote the equations of electromagnetism in it, and Einstein wrote relativity in it.
And every modern physics simulation still runs on it today.

## 1799 to 1831 — the complex plane

Now, back to the imaginary numbers.
Two hundred years after Cardano, they were still treated as a trick.
Until an idea came along that was surprisingly simple.

In seventeen ninety-nine Caspar Wessel, and in eighteen oh six Jean-Robert Argand, showed how to picture them.
A few decades later, Gauss made the idea standard.
The idea was this: take the one-dimensional number line, and widen it into a two-dimensional plane.
The horizontal axis holds the real numbers, and the vertical axis holds the imaginary ones.
Every point in that plane is a complex number.
And multiplying by i? It is simply a quarter turn.

Suddenly, the mystery was gone.
And then it turned out that complex numbers are the natural language of waves, vibrations, alternating current, and in the end, all of quantum mechanics.
The Schrödinger equation, which describes how an electron behaves, has the letter i right inside it.
No complex numbers, no modern physics.

# ACT FIVE — Questioning the foundations

## 1829 — non-Euclidean geometry

Take a sheet of paper, draw a straight line, and mark a point that is not on it.
Here is the question: how many lines through that point never meet the first line?
Everybody knows the answer. One. Exactly one.
And that is Euclid's fifth axiom, the one we promised would come back.

But there was always something odd about it.
Euclid's first four axioms are short and obvious.
The fifth is long and fussy, and it looks like something that should be proved, not simply assumed.
For two thousand years, one mathematician after another tried to prove it from the other four.
Nobody managed.

Then, around eighteen twenty-nine, the Russian mathematician Nikolai Lobachevsky gave an answer nobody had dared to give.
It can't be proved, because it doesn't follow from the others.
You can keep it, or you can replace it with its opposite, and either way, you get a geometry in which no contradiction has ever been found.

To picture it, let the surface bend.
On flat paper, there is exactly one parallel through the point.
But on a surface curved like a saddle, there are infinitely many lines through the point that never meet the first.
And on a sphere, there are none at all.
Zero, one, or infinitely many: different worlds, different answers, and each one is right in its own world.

At the same time, János Bolyai in Hungary reached the same conclusion on his own.
And Gauss, in Germany, had seen it even earlier, but kept it to himself, afraid of the reaction.
This was more than a technical result.
For the first time in history, people realised that axioms are not eternal truths.
They are choices.
Choose different axioms, and you get a different mathematical universe.

## 1854 — Riemann: curved space

In eighteen fifty-four, Bernhard Riemann gave a famous lecture at the University of Göttingen, and took the new geometry much further.
He didn't just change the parallel axiom.
He built a framework for doing geometry on any curved space, in any number of dimensions.
On a sphere, on a saddle, in four dimensions, in eleven.

It sounded like pure abstraction.
But sixty years later, Einstein used exactly this geometry to describe how gravity curves space.
General relativity exists, because long before, someone dared to question Euclid's fifth axiom.

## 1874 — Cantor: sizes of infinity

In eighteen seventy-four, Georg Cantor published a result that almost nobody at the time was ready to accept.
He compared infinities with each other.

Before Cantor, infinity was a single idea, and it couldn't be divided up.
Cantor proved the opposite: there are different sizes of infinity, and some are bigger than others.
The counting numbers, one, two, three, and so on, go on forever.
The real numbers, including pi, the square root of two, and every endless decimal, go on forever too.
But the infinity of the real numbers is genuinely larger.

In eighteen ninety-one, he gave a proof so short and so elegant that it is still taught everywhere: the diagonal argument.
List real numbers in any order you like, one for each counting number.
Now build a new number, by changing the first digit of the first, the second digit of the second, and so on, all the way down the diagonal.
That new number is different from every number on your list.
So however you try to pair them up, there is always a real number left over.

Many of his colleagues were furious, and one of them called him a corrupter of youth.
But in those same years, Cantor and others built set theory, a common language in which every branch of mathematics can be written.
Numbers are sets. Functions are sets. Shapes are sets.
Modern mathematics was rebuilt on a foundation of sets.

## 1901 — Russell's paradox

But Cantor's first, informal idea of a set had a hole in it.
In nineteen oh one, the British philosopher and mathematician Bertrand Russell found it, with one simple question.

Take the set of all sets that do not contain themselves.
Does that set contain itself?
If it does, then by its own definition, it must not.
If it doesn't, then by its own definition, it must.
A contradiction, either way.

One question, on one page, was enough to show that the naive idea of a set was inconsistent.
After Russell, mathematicians had to rebuild set theory on stricter axioms, careful about which sets are allowed to exist.
And the greatest mathematician of the age, David Hilbert, set out a bold plan: to prove, once and for all, that mathematics is complete and consistent.
In nineteen thirty, he told an audience: we must know, and we will know.

## 1931 — Gödel: incompleteness

But the day before that speech, in the same city, a quiet twenty-four-year-old had already answered him.
His name was Kurt Gödel, an Austrian logician, and he published his proof in nineteen thirty-one.

Put simply, he proved this.
Any consistent system of axioms that is strong enough for arithmetic contains true statements that it cannot prove.
And such a system can never prove its own consistency.

This was not a flaw in one particular theory.
It is a limit on the very idea the Greeks invented twenty-three hundred years earlier: proof from axioms.
Mathematics can be true, but it can never certify itself as both complete and consistent.

# ACT SIX — The machine

## 1936 — Turing

Hilbert had one more question.
Is there a mechanical procedure that can decide, for any statement, whether it can be proved?
To answer it, a young Cambridge mathematician first had to pin down what a mechanical procedure actually is.

In nineteen thirty-six, Alan Turing described an imaginary machine.
An endless tape, divided into squares.
A head that reads one mark at a time, writes a mark, and moves one square left or right.
And a short table of rules.
That's all.

Turing, and independently Alonzo Church, proposed that anything that can be computed by a finite, mechanical procedure can be computed by such a machine.
With it, Turing answered Hilbert: no. Some questions can never be decided by any procedure.
And in the same stroke, he drew the limit of everything any machine will ever be able to do.

A few years later, engineers began building the first electronic computers.
And every computer since then, from the room-sized machines of the nineteen forties, to the phone in your pocket, to the data centres running modern AI, is a Turing machine made real.
After thousands of years living only on paper and in people's heads, mathematics finally had a physical body.

## Looking back

Thirty thousand years ago, someone scratched marks into a bone.
Today, a machine reads marks on a tape, billions of times a second.
And between the two runs one single thread.
In every age, people widened what they were willing to call valid.

At first, there were only counting numbers.
Then came zero, negative numbers, fractions, irrational numbers, complex numbers, infinity, and then many infinities.
At first, there was only flat geometry.
Then curved geometry, then spaces with many dimensions, then spaces no one can even picture.
At first, proof was everything.
Then Gödel showed that even proof has limits of its own.

Mathematics is not a fixed set of rules that someone pressed onto reality.
It is a building that is still under construction.
Each generation adds a new floor, and every new floor forces the old ones to take another look at themselves.

And you are living at a moment when that building is still going up.
Some problems posed centuries ago are still unsolved, and Riemann himself left one behind, in eighteen fifty-nine, that nobody has cracked yet.
Some branches of mathematics are only a few decades old.
And quite possibly, the next great mathematical invention hasn't been thought of yet.
